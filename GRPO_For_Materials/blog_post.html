Reinforcement Learning for Crystal Relaxation

By jmedina9 • 12 min read • RL Environments

Finding the stable state of a crystal—a process known as structural relaxation—is the bedrock of computational materials science. In density functional theory (DFT), a typical geometry optimization (BFGS or conjugate gradient) requires dozens to hundreds of single-point energy and force evaluations, each solving the Kohn–Sham equations self-consistently at O(N3) cost. For high-throughput screening across thousands of candidate structures, this is prohibitive. This project asks: can a language model, trained with reinforcement learning, learn to propose relaxed structures in a single forward pass—replacing the entire iterative SCF loop?

The Challenge: The Energy Landscape

In a standard DFT relaxation pipeline, a geometry optimizer such as BFGS or conjugate gradient iteratively displaces atoms along the negative force gradient until ‖F‖ < ε (typically 0.01–0.05 eV/Å). While precise, this requires an energy and force evaluation at every step—prohibitively expensive for large-scale screening. Universal neural network potentials (UNNPs) like CHGNet replace the DFT calculator with a GNN surrogate, but they still rely on the same local gradient-following logic and get trapped in local minima for disordered inputs.

By framing relaxation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) M = (S, A, T, R, γ), we can train an LLM policy πθ(a | s) to navigate the PES in a single forward pass rather than through iterative gradient descent:

  • S: Crystal structure encoded as a CIF string (lattice parameters a, b, c, α, β, γ + fractional atomic coordinates)
  • A: A new CIF string output by the LLM (atomic displacements + optional lattice strain)
  • T: Deterministic—the next state is exactly the CIF the agent outputs, parsed and re-evaluated
  • R: Weighted rubric score from CHGNet (energy, forces, bond lengths, composition, format)

Environment Highlights:

  • State Space: CIF string encoding lattice vectors (a, b, c, α, β, γ), space group, and fractional Wyckoff coordinates for each atom. Dimensionality scales with system size (typically 3N + 6 degrees of freedom).
  • Action Space: Discrete token space over CIF text. Effectively encodes continuous atomic displacements (δx, δy, δz per atom) and lattice strain tensors (εij) through text generation.
  • Reward: Weighted sum R = 0.10·rfmt + 0.15·rcomp + 0.60·rEf + 0.24·rF + 0.15·rbond, computed by a CHGNet inference server wrapped in the Prime Intellect verifiers library.
  • Training algorithm: GRPO—samples G rollouts per prompt and optimizes the policy relative to the group mean reward, without a learned critic.

The Dataset

The training environment uses a curated subset of the Materials Project mp-20 benchmark [3], originally compiled by Chen et al. (2021). The dataset is downloaded directly from HuggingFace: 4everStudent/mp-20-relaxation-dataset-with-clusters. MP-20 contains ~45,000 experimentally reported inorganic crystal structures with up to 20 atoms per unit cell, spanning 89 elements across the periodic table.

Filtering criteria: To restrict the agent's search space to thermodynamically accessible materials, I applied a hard threshold of Ef < 0.1 eV/atom on the DFT formation energies from the Materials Project. This retains structures that lie within the commonly accepted convex hull tolerance for synthesizability [4], discarding high-energy metastable phases that would produce noisy reward signals during early training.

Dataset Statistics (filtered subset)

  • Total structures: ~45,000 (full mp-20) → ~28,000 after Ef filter
  • Training pool used: 2,000 randomly sampled structures
  • Average atoms/cell: 8.3
  • Space groups covered: 148 (out of 230 possible)
  • Formation energy range: −3.9 to 0.1 eV/atom
  • Composition diversity: unary through quaternary systems

🔮 The Model: Fine-tuned CHGNet

At the heart of this environment is CHGNet (Crystal Hamiltonian Graph Neural Network) [1], a universal MLIP pretrained on ~1.5 million Materials Project DFT calculations. CHGNet is a message-passing GNN that operates on crystal graphs where nodes are atoms and edges encode pairwise interactions within a cutoff radius (default 5 Å). A key architectural feature is the explicit inclusion of magnetic moments as node features alongside atomic species and fractional coordinates—enabling CHGNet to distinguish spin states that conventional force fields miss.

The model predicts four targets simultaneously: total energy E, per-atom forces F = −∂E/∂r, virial stress tensor σ, and site magnetic moments m. This multi-task formulation is critical for RL reward computation: we use E for the formation energy rubric and ‖F‖ for the forces rubric.

To reduce systematic bias on the filtered mp-20 subset, I fine-tuned the pretrained CHGNet checkpoint:

Fine-tuning Configuration:

  • Training samples: 2000 structures from the filtered mp-20 subset
  • Targets: Formation energy per atom + atomic forces
  • Batch size: 8
  • Epochs: 10
  • Optimizer: Adam with learning rate 1e-2
  • Loss function: Mean Squared Error (MSE)
  • Scheduler: Cosine annealing learning rate
  • Train/Val/Test split: 90% / 5% / 5%

Fine-tuning reduces the systematic offset (bias) that the pretrained model accumulates when evaluated on the low-energy filtered subset—the baseline CHGNet was trained on the full Materials Project including high-energy structures, so its prediction distribution is shifted. After fine-tuning, the MAE on formation energy dropped from ~0.21 eV/atom (baseline) to ~0.08 eV/atom on the test split, keeping reward noise within the 0.1 eV/atom threshold that defines our stability criterion.

4. Fine-tune the Model

trainer.train(train_loader, val_loader, test_loader)

# Save the fine-tuned model
torch.save(trainer.best_model.state_dict(), "fine_tuned_chgnet_model.pth")

The RL Environment: Multi-Turn Crystal Relaxation

The crystal relaxation task is framed as a MultiTurnEnv—a multi-step RL environment built on the Prime Intellect verifiers library. At each turn t, the LLM policy receives the full conversation history (initial structure + all previous proposals and rubric feedback) as context and outputs a new CIF. This is analogous to a chain-of-thought policy where the "scratchpad" is the turn history.

Training uses GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization): for each prompt, G = 8 rollouts are sampled from the current policy πθ, their rewards are normalized within the group (subtract mean, divide by std), and the policy is updated to increase the probability of above-average rollouts. No value network is needed—the group statistics serve as an implicit baseline, reducing variance without introducing a separate critic.

🎯 Objective

Given a disordered initial structure, the agent must generate a valid CIF file that:

  • Has valid crystallographic format
  • Matches the target composition
  • Achieves formation energy below 0.1 eV/atom
  • Minimizes atomic forces (well-relaxed)
  • Maintains physically reasonable bond lengths

How It Works

1️⃣
Initial State: The environment provides a disordered crystal structure from the MP20 dataset with high formation energy.
2️⃣
Agent Action: The LLM agent generates a response containing:
  • Reasoning about structural improvements (in <|think_start|>...<|think_end|>)
  • A proposed CIF structure (in <|answer_start|>...<|answer_end|>)
3️⃣
Evaluation: CHGNet evaluates the proposed structure across multiple rubrics, computing energies, forces, and bond lengths.
4️⃣
Feedback: The environment provides detailed feedback on what passed/failed, guiding the agent toward better structures in subsequent turns.

Reward Structure

The total reward is a weighted sum of individual rubric scores:

Rubric Weight Criteria
Format 0.10 Valid CIF syntax
Composition 0.15 Matches target formula
Formation Energy 0.60 < 0.1 eV/atom
Forces 0.24 Low atomic forces
Bond Lengths 0.15 Physically reasonable

Note: Formation energy has the highest weight (60%) as it's the primary indicator of structural stability.

📄 Format Rubric (Weight: 0.10)

Ensures the agent produces syntactically valid CIF files and properly uses the answer tags.

What It Checks:

  • CIF string is present between <|answer_start|> and <|answer_end|> tags
  • CIF can be parsed by pymatgen's CifParser
  • Structure contains valid crystallographic data
  • No critical syntax errors in the CIF format

Scoring:

if CIF successfully parsed:
    reward = 1.0 * weight (0.10)
else:
    reward = 0.0

Why it matters: Without a valid CIF, the structure cannot be evaluated by CHGNet. This rubric ensures the agent learns the proper format before focusing on energy optimization.

⚗️ Composition Rubric (Weight: 0.15)

Verifies that the generated structure has the correct chemical composition in the right ratios. This rubric serves two distinct roles: (1) it provides an easy gradient signal early in training, when the model is too weak to consistently pass the stability rubrics—preventing the policy from receiving zero reward on every step; and (2) it acts as an anti-reward-hacking mechanism, ensuring the agent cannot achieve high scores by memorizing a single simple crystal that passes energy/force thresholds regardless of the requested composition.

What It Checks:

  • Composition matches the target formula (e.g., NaCl, Li₂O)
  • Element ratios are correct (reduced composition)
  • No extra or missing elements

Implementation:

# Uses pymatgen's ElementComparator
cif_composition = structure.composition.reduced_composition
target_composition = Composition(target_formula).reduced_composition

if ElementComparator().are_equal(cif_composition, target_composition):
    reward = 1.0 * weight (0.15)
else:
    reward = 0.0

Why it matters: The chemical composition fundamentally determines material properties—an agent that generates NaCl₂ instead of NaCl has failed at a basic chemistry level. Keeping weight low (0.15 vs. 0.60 for energy) prevents the agent from gaming composition alone, but non-zero weight ensures the rubric provides early training signal before the harder stability objectives become reachable.

⚡ Formation Energy Rubric (Weight: 0.60)

The most important rubric—evaluates thermodynamic stability through formation energy.

What It Checks:

  • Formation energy per atom < 0.1 eV/atom (threshold)
  • Calculated using fine-tuned CHGNet model
  • Lower energy = more stable structure = higher reward

Available Reward Strategies

The environment supports three different reward functions to guide the optimization process:

1. Discrete Reward (Step Function)

Binary feedback. Simple but provides sparse gradients.

def formation_energy_discrete(..., threshold=0.1):
    if energy <= threshold:
        return 1.0
    return 0.0
2. Linear Reward (Default)
Recommended

Continuous feedback. Decays linearly as energy rises above threshold.

def formation_energy_linear(..., threshold=0.1, max_penalty=2.0):
    if energy <= threshold:
        return 1.0
    elif energy >= threshold + max_penalty:
        return 0.0
    else:
        # Interpolate 1.0 -> 0.0
        return 1.0 - (energy - threshold) / max_penalty
3. Exponential Reward

Aggressive penalty for high energy structures.

def formation_energy_exponential(..., threshold=0.1, scale=1.0):
    if energy <= threshold:
        return 1.0
    excess = energy - threshold
    return np.exp(-excess / scale)

⚠️ Critical Threshold

Structures with formation energy above 0.1 eV/atom are considered thermodynamically unstable and unlikely to exist naturally. The agent must learn to minimize this value through proper atomic positioning and lattice parameters.

Why it matters: Formation energy is the gold standard for predicting whether a material can be synthesized. This 60% weight ensures the agent prioritizes structural stability above all else.

🔧 Forces Rubric (Weight: 0.24)

Measures how well-relaxed the structure is by examining forces on individual atoms.

What It Checks:

  • Maximum force on any atom < threshold (typically 0.5 eV/Å)
  • Average force magnitude across all atoms
  • Indicates if atoms are in equilibrium positions

Available Reward Strategies

The environment provides three ways to reward structural relaxation:

1. Discrete Reward

Strict binary check. Only rewards if the maximum force on any atom is below threshold.

def forces_discrete(..., threshold=0.05):
    # forces = get_forces(structure)
    max_force = np.max(np.linalg.norm(forces, axis=1))
    return 1.0 if max_force <= threshold else 0.0
2. Continuous Decay (Default)
Recommended

Smoothly penalizes larger forces. Even imperfect structures get some credit, guiding the gradient.

def forces_continuous_decay(..., decay=1.0):
    # forces = get_forces(structure)
    max_force = np.max(np.linalg.norm(forces, axis=1))
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + (decay * max_force))
3. Continuous Ratio

Rewards the percentage of well-behaved atoms. Good for large structures.

def forces_continuous_ratio(..., threshold=0.05):
    # forces = get_forces(structure)
    force_norms = np.linalg.norm(forces, axis=1)
    good_forces = np.sum(force_norms <= threshold)
    return good_forces / len(force_norms)

Physical intuition: In a stable structure, atoms sit at energy minima where forces are near zero. Large forces indicate atoms "want" to move—the structure hasn't truly relaxed.

💡 Agent learning: This rubric teaches the agent that low formation energy alone isn't enough—the structure must also be mechanically stable with minimal residual forces.

📏 Bond Lengths Rubric (Weight: 0.15)

Validates that interatomic distances are chemically reasonable based on atomic radii and bond types.

What It Checks:

  • Bond lengths fall within expected ranges (±32% tolerance)
  • Accounts for ionic vs covalent bonding (electronegativity difference)
  • Special handling for hydrogen bonds (more flexible)
  • Uses pymatgen's CrystalNN to identify bonded atoms

Available Reward Strategies

You can configure the strictness of the bond length check:

1. Discrete Reward

Binary accept/reject. Only rewards structures where the global bond score exceeds a threshold.

def bond_lengths_discrete(..., threshold=0.75):
    # score = bond_length_reasonableness_score(structure)
    if not score or score < 0:
        return 0.0
    return 1.0 if score >= threshold else 0.0
2. Continuous Reward (Default)
Recommended

Returns the raw reasonableness score (0.0 to 1.0), encouraging incremental improvements.

def bond_lengths_continuous(..., **kwargs):
    # score = bond_length_reasonableness_score(structure)
    if not score or score < 0:
        return 0.0
    # Returns raw score 0.0 - 1.0
    return max(0.0, min(1.0, score))

Common Failures:

  • Atoms too close → unphysical overlap
  • Atoms too far → weak/nonexistent bonding
  • Incorrect lattice parameters → compressed/expanded structure

Why it matters: Even if CHGNet predicts low energy, physically impossible bond lengths indicate the structure is chemically unrealistic. This rubric acts as a sanity check on the agent's geometric proposals.

What's Next?

Several open problems are worth pursuing from here:

  1. Vector feedback for iterative learning. Current feedback is scalar ("energy too high"). The training analysis shows the agent can't fix failed structures, only re-roll. Providing per-atom force vectors as textual feedback—e.g., "atom 3 (O) has residual force 1.2 eV/Å along c"—could give the policy the gradient signal it needs for true iterative refinement.
  2. Curriculum over Ef threshold. Start with a wide threshold (0.5 eV/atom) and anneal it toward 0.05 eV/atom as training progresses—analogous to curriculum learning in RL for locomotion tasks. This avoids the sparse reward problem at initialization.
  3. Generalization to novel compositions. The current agent is trained on mp-20 structures. Testing on out-of-distribution materials (e.g., high-entropy alloys, ternary nitrides outside the training set) would probe whether the policy has learned generalizable structural heuristics or is pattern-matching mp-20 CIFs.
  4. Symmetry-aware action space. All atomic coordinates are currently treated independently. Imposing Wyckoff position constraints during generation—enforcing the correct space group symmetry in the CIF—would dramatically reduce the effective action space and should improve sample efficiency.
  5. Hybrid LLM + optimizer pipeline. Use the LLM as a coarse-grained proposer and run a CHGNet-driven BFGS pass on the output for fine-grained relaxation. This combines the generative flexibility of an LLM policy with the convergence guarantees of gradient-based optimization.

⚠️ What Didn't Work

These are the failure modes discovered during development—documented so others don’t repeat them.

1. Equal weighting of composition and stability rewards leads to reward hacking

In an early version of the reward function, composition and formation energy rubrics were given equal weight (~0.40 each). The agent quickly discovered it could achieve consistently high rewards by memorizing a single structurally simple crystal (e.g., a binary oxide with symmetric Wyckoff positions) that reliably scores well on both metrics regardless of the target composition. This is a failure mode unique to multi-objective reward design: the agent exploits the easiest rubric to compensate for failing the harder one. The fix was to make composition a low-weight auxiliary signal (0.15) while keeping it non-zero—low enough that the policy cannot hack it alone, but high enough to provide gradient signal early in training when the model is too weak to pass the stability rubric. The current weighting (60% energy, 24% forces, 15% composition, 10% format, 15% bonds) was tuned to prevent this collapse.

2. Loading CHGNet directly on GPU workers causes out-of-memory (OOM) errors

In the initial setup, the CHGNet model was loaded inside each reward function call on the same GPU running the LLM policy. With 8 rollouts per prompt and the LLM already consuming most of the GPU VRAM, loading CHGNet (>500 MB) on-device pushed the process over the memory limit and triggered CUDA OOM errors mid-rollout. The fix was to serve CHGNet as a separate FastAPI microservice (uvicorn on a dedicated port), so reward computation runs on CPU in isolation. The environment now auto-detects tmux and launches the server in a background pane on port 1111 by default; if tmux is unavailable, it falls back to a subprocess call on CPU. This decoupling also makes the reward function stateless and independently testable.

Training Runs: How an LLM “Learns” Physics

Posted on February 17, 2026 • Tagged: RL Materials Science

We ran the crystal relaxation environment on two model scales: a 30B-parameter model (87 rollout samples, steps 0–100, 11 checkpoints) and a 230B-parameter model (63 samples, steps 0–070, 8 checkpoints). Both runs use the same reward function, the same filtered MP-20 dataset from HuggingFace, and the same GRPO training loop. What we found is that scale doesn’t just change how well a model learns—it determines what kind of reasoning strategy RL selects for entirely.

The single most dramatic data point: at step 30, the 30B model produces the only zero-reward sample in either dataset (SnF₃, where the model correctly identifies a symmetry mismatch but fails to produce any valid output).

These are not just different amounts; they are opposite directions. System 1 vs. System 2 Reasoning

This maps cleanly onto Kahneman’s dual-process framework. The 230B model develops System 2 reasoning: deliberative, step-by-step crystallographic analysis that deepens over training. By step 30, it correctly identifies hexagonal cells from γ = 120°; by step 60–70, it references Wyckoff positions, prototype structures, and Pnma space groups it was never explicitly trained on—and explicitly acknowledges it cannot run real DFT while still producing near-perfect structures.

The 30B model converges on System 1: compressed, procedural preambles (“relaxation will be performed using DFT—output below”) that discard domain vocabulary in favor of getting directly to the CIF. RL selects for this compression because the smaller model lacks the capacity to maintain both elaborate reasoning and correct structural output simultaneously. By step 90–100, the reasoning reads as a formality rather than a problem-solving step—the model even inserts the parenthetical “(simulated here for brevity)”.

The keyword data confirms this divergence. In the 230B model, “symmetry” mentions grow from 6.4 per sample at step 0 to 11.3 at step 60, and Wyckoff positions emerge at step 40+. In the 30B model, “symmetry” starts at 0.6 and reaches 0.0 by step 100. “Bond” drops from 2.0 to 0.0 by step 60. “Coordination” and “Wyckoff” never appear at any step.

3. The “Bond” Paradox

The 30B model’s “bond” keyword count drops from 2.0 per sample at step 0 to 0.0 by step 60—and stays at zero through step 100. On the surface, this looks like the model forgetting about bond lengths.

In reality, the bond length reward improves from 0.574 to 0.809 over the same period (peaking at 0.818 at step 90). The model’s bond quality gets better while it stops talking about bonds. This is the compression heuristic in action: the concept has been internalized into the structural output itself, removed from the scratchpad. The 30B model learns to do without needing to say. The 230B model does the opposite: bond mentions also decline after step 50, but from a higher baseline and after the model has already peak-loaded its symmetry vocabulary.

4. Conservative Perturbations—Two Very Different Kinds

Both models learn to make smaller lattice adjustments over time through step 60. But the degree, pattern, and outcome differ sharply:

Model Δa at Step 0 Min Δa Reduction FE score at reward peak
230B 0.053 Å 0.017 Å (step 60) −68% 0.957 (step 60)
30B 0.025 Å 0.002 Å (step 90) −91% 0.658 (step 80)

The 230B model’s conservatism is healthy: it mirrors real DFT relaxation behavior, correlates with excellent formation energy scores, and notably rebounds at step 70 (Δa = 0.084 Å)—the model becomes more forceful once it has the structural reasoning to support larger corrections, and bond quality peaks at 0.938 that same step. The 30B model’s conservatism is pathological: modifying the lattice by 0.002 Å is essentially returning the input unchanged. It avoids catastrophic bond-length violations but cannot correct misplaced lattice parameters—hence formation energies that remain 10–20× above the 0.1 eV/atom threshold throughout all 100 steps.

5. Formation Energy: The Capacity Bottleneck

The formation energy reward component is where the capacity gap is most visible. The 230B model reaches a formation energy score of 0.957 at step 60 and sustains 0.896 at step 70. The 30B model peaks at 0.658 at step 80 and falls back to 0.283 by step 100—a ~45-percentage-point gap at peak, widening to 61 points by the end of each run.

In absolute terms, the 30B model’s generated structures have mean formation energies oscillating between 0.93 and 1.86 eV/atom across all steps—10–20× above the 0.1 eV/atom stability threshold. The structures pass composition and bond-length checks, but they are not genuinely relaxed. The model has learned to be a competent CIF formatter and a cautious structure perturber, but not a crystal-structure optimizer.

6. The Failure Mode: The “Retry” Illusion

In multi-turn rollouts, when the first attempt fails the energy threshold, the model gets a second turn with the validator’s feedback. Analysis of 37 first-turn failures from the 30B run:

  • Physical recoveries (energy, forces, bonds): 0. Zero cases of fixing bad physics.
  • Syntax recoveries (format corrections): 7. The model can fix a missing CIF line, but not a bad structure.

The model treats scalar feedback (“Formation energy is too high”) as a signal to re-roll rather than to diagnose. It rephrases its reasoning and outputs a near-identical CIF with minor random perturbations. It has learned state recognition (what a valid crystal looks like) but not gradient estimation (which direction to move atoms to lower energy).

There is one exception: prediction improves over turns. Even though the model cannot fix a bad structure, its ability to estimate formation energy from the feedback context improves in the second turn—an “Actor-Critic” disconnect where the Critic (prediction) improves while the Actor (structure generation) stalls.

“The model learns to measure the problem before it learns to fix it.”

What this implies for the next run:

Scalar feedback (“energy too high”) provides no directional gradient in atom-coordinate space. The path forward is vector feedback—per-atom force vectors, specific bond-length violations, which Wyckoff site is strained—so the policy has an actual direction to move. The 230B model shows that with sufficient capacity, RL can induce genuinely calibrated structural reasoning. Whether a 30B model can exploit richer feedback to close the gap, or whether the formation energy bottleneck is a hard capacity limit, is the central open question.
